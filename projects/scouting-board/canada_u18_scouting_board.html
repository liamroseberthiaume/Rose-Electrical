How these rankings were built ▾

The Current Board Consensus

Built from published 2027 and 2028 draft rankings — Elite Prospects' top 64, Daily Faceoff's July 2026 top 32, TSN's Craig Button, NHL.com's lookahead — then adjusted for what actually happened in Edmonton. It is a map of perception, not a verdict.

Because two draft classes are mixed here, players are placed on absolute prospect value rather than rank-within-class. A projected first overall pick in 2028 outranks a projected 20th pick in 2027.

The Ceiling Board Projection

Same players, re-sorted by two historical patterns that have repeatedly separated the prospects who exceeded expectations from the ones who did not. This is an upside sort: it asks how high a player could get, not who I would actually call.

Neither model is predictive on its own. Together they identify the two errors that show up most reliably in retrospect: mistaking older for better, and mistaking bigger now for better later.

The GM Board Decision

The order I would actually draft in, with my job riding on it. Same evidence as the ceiling board, run through a risk rule rather than a pure upside sort — because the highest ceiling and the right pick are not the same list.

Position-neutral: best player available, goaltenders included, with no scarcity premium for centres and no automatic discount for the crease. Ranked on where a player ends up, not on how soon he gets there.

Pattern 1 — Age-adjusted production

The NHL's 15 September cutoff means a January-born prospect can be nearly nine months older than an August-born one in the same class. At 16, that gap is enormous — and scouts price it in far too weakly.

Deaner et al., PLOS One (2013): Q4-born players were 14.5% of Canadian draftees from 1980–2006 but played 20% of the games and scored 19% of the points. Controlling for draft slot, they reached 400 games at 25% vs 13% for Q1-born players. Relatively younger players needed to be drafted 40–45 slots earlier to be valued correctly.
Frontiers in Sports (2025), 10,485 draftees: Q1 births were 35.7% of all picks, Q4 just 15.4% — yet 21% of Q4 draftees achieved career permanence against 15% of Q1, and 50% of Q4 never played an NHL game against 60% of Q1.

Applied here as points-per-game in top junior, weighted by exact age and league. Six players on this roster are 2028-eligible and were producing against opponents up to two years older.

Pattern 2 — The physical maturity curve

A U18 national team is precisely the selection event that over-picks early-maturing bodies. The strongest longitudinal evidence says that advantage reverses.

Sports Medicine (2023), 4,787 Swedish players tracked 20 years: early maturers made up 30% of U16 national team selections and late maturers 19%. Among the players who eventually reached the NHL, early maturers were 25% and late maturers 40%. NHL players were measurably later-maturing at 16.
Hockey Graphs (2022): scouts systematically overvalue size and non-scoring traits. Low-scoring prospects are drafted "one entire draft group too early," while point production — which explains roughly half the variance in NHL forward value — is underweighted.

Applied here as maturity headroom: current mass relative to frame and role, plus scouting language about strength. A 150 lb forward producing in the WHL has somewhere to go. A 201 lb 16-year-old winning on leverage has already spent his advantage.

The rule the GM board runs on Asymmetric risk

A draft board is not a talent ranking, it is a sequence of decisions, and the question at every slot is not "who is best?" but "what does passing on this player actually buy me?" Three tests, applied in order:

One — is the safe alternative genuinely safe? Take the volatile player when the conservative option's realistic outcome is no better than the volatile player's failure case. Low variance around a mediocre result is not safety, it is a worse bet wearing a disguise.

Two — is the case evidence or is it decoration? Production, at a known age, in a known league, is evidence. A skating grade, a frame, a pedigree, a ranking someone else published — those are decoration until something confirms them. Cases built on decoration fall.

Three — absent evidence is not bad evidence. A player with four games of tape is unknown; a player with a full season of weak numbers is known and disappointing. They do not get the same treatment.

The rule, worked The two goaltenders

Leif Oaten looks like the safe pick. He carried a 31-game WHL starter's load at sixteen, won Canada's crease outright and started the gold-medal game. Sam Berthiaume has one QMJHL appearance and sat behind him in Edmonton.

Test one: Oaten's .876 is roughly 15–25 points below WHL average. Berthiaume's .927 is roughly 30–40 points above MJAHL average — in a men's league, at sixteen. Oaten's 6-19-2 team excuses some of his number and Berthiaume's 12-4-0 team flatters some of his. Adjust both and the gap narrows without closing.

The safe pick's floor is a junior goaltender who never posted an above-average season. That is not a floor worth protecting. Meanwhile 6'5" at 185 lb is the largest strength runway on the roster, and Berthiaume's save percentage is strong at nearly every level he has played.

So Berthiaume goes twelfth and Oaten seventeenth — a ten-place rise off a consensus that had Berthiaume last. The risk being bought knowingly: tall-and-light is the specific goaltending archetype that never coordinates.

Overall rating (0–99). Anchored to this roster only. The best player here is a 99; every other rating is that player's distance from him within this group. It is not a global birth-year rating — a 76 on this board is still one of the better 17-year-olds in the world.

Tool grades (1–10). Projected NHL grade for that tool at maturity, not current level. 5 is NHL average, 8+ is a top-of-league tool, 3 or below is a genuine limitation. Goaltenders use their own five tools.

Three ratings per player. Consensus, ceiling, and my board. Where they diverge is the whole point: consensus versus ceiling shows what the market is mispricing, ceiling versus my board shows where I would not spend a pick even though the upside is real.

Caveat worth stating plainly. These are 16- and 17-year-olds. Two of them are goaltenders, where prediction at this age is barely better than noise. All three boards are sets of falsifiable claims, not forecasts — the value is in seeing where consensus and evidence pull apart, and checking back in two years.
